# Insulate your home

Home insulation plays a critical role in keeping a New Zealand home warmer, cutting heating costs, and improving energy efficiency. EECA estimates that ensuring a home is well insulated can save up to NZ$330 per year on power bills. Insulation works best when it forms a continuous "thermal envelope" around the home — ceilings, walls and floors together. In older homes this is harder to achieve, so EECA recommends starting with the areas that are easiest to access, or insulating during other renovation work.

## Quick checks

- **Floors:** confirm insulation hasn't slipped out of place, remains clean and dry, and is the correct type for the sub-floor space.
- **Ceilings:** insulation should be dry, clean, and provide good coverage at a thickness of at least 120mm; replace or top up if needed.
- **Walls:** if wall cavities are being opened for renovation work, that is the ideal opportunity for an expert to check or retrofit wall insulation.

## Ceiling insulation

Ceilings are usually the easiest and most effective part of an existing home to insulate. Existing insulation settles and compresses over time, losing effectiveness; if it is still dry and in decent condition, a new layer can often be added on top.

Signs insulation needs replacing or topping up:

- less than 120mm thick, or sitting visibly below the ceiling joists;
- gaps in coverage (even small breaks allow significant heat loss);
- poor condition — dampness, mould, or soiling from pests or leaks.

Other ceiling considerations:

- **Roofs with no ceiling cavity** (cathedral, skillion or flat roofs) are difficult to insulate unless the roof or ceiling lining is being replaced; where retrofitting is possible, keep at least a 25mm gap between insulation and roofing underlay to avoid condensation and moisture problems.
- **Older downlights:** gaps in insulation may be required around older downlight fittings due to fire risk from bulb heat. Some modern LED bulbs can safely have insulation fitted over them, and use less energy.
- **Product types:** *blankets* (rolls that cover both ceiling lining and joists, good for top-ups and for reducing heat loss through timber); *segments* (cut-to-size pieces fitted between joists — they leave joists exposed, which helps access but lets heat escape through the timber, so choose a higher-density product); *loose-fill* (blown-in, useful where access is tight, but performance depends heavily on material quality and installer skill).
- **Minimum requirements** exist for rental homes, new homes and alterations (see Tenancy Services' healthy homes insulation standard: https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/healthy-homes/insulation-standard/). Use these as a floor for retrofits and go higher where possible.

## Wall insulation

Wall cavities are part of the thermal envelope but harder to access than roofs or under-floor spaces, making retrofits more complex — though still worthwhile for long-term savings and comfort. EECA recommends a qualified professional: wall insulation typically requires building consent and consideration of cladding, wiring and moisture protection. The best time to insulate walls is when cavities are already open — e.g. when replacing cladding or internal linings. If building paper is absent or damaged, install it at the same time to protect the insulation from moisture.

## Underfloor insulation

Insulating between the floor and the ground is one of the easiest, most cost-effective comfort upgrades. If the sub-floor is more than half a metre off the ground, insulation fits easily between the joists; it is often worth doing at the same time as the ceiling. Replace existing underfloor insulation that has slipped or has gaps.

Sub-floor considerations:

- **Exposed sub-floor spaces:** wind and weather degrade insulation; protect it by enclosing the sub-floor (in line with the building code) or fixing plywood or fibre-cement sheets to the underside of the floor after installation.
- **Enclosed sub-floor spaces:** laying a polythene ground sheet on the soil beneath the house reduces moisture, helps the insulation perform, and makes the home healthier and easier to heat.
- **Limited access:** insulate when floor work (repiling, replacing floorboards) is already happening.
- **Product choice:** usually bulk fibre or polystyrene, fitted hard against the underfloor with no gaps or sagging; joist spacing varies, so confirm width. For DIY, choose a product intended as underfloor insulation with an R-value of at least R1.4 (higher preferred); if the space is not fully enclosed, the product must be rated for windy conditions.
- **Concrete slabs:** the slab itself cannot be insulated, but its thermal mass provides some insulating effect; the thermal envelope can be improved by insulating around the slab perimeter with vertical strips.
- **Foil-based insulation (safety):** many older NZ homes used aluminium foil insulation; retrofitting or repairing it is now banned. If found, turn off the power supply before inspecting, involve an electrician if unsure, and have damaged foil carefully removed (power off) and replaced with modern bulk underfloor insulation. See WorkSafe's electricity codes of practice: https://www.worksafe.govt.nz/laws-and-regulations/electrical-and-gas-codes-of-practice/electricity-codes-of-practice/

## Insulation types and R-values

The R-value indicates how effective insulation is; higher R-values mean better resistance to winter heat loss. Some materials achieve a given R-value with less thickness; others must be thicker or denser. In New Zealand, insulation products should comply with AS/NZS 4859.1 and include safe-handling instructions and performance guarantees. A professional installer can help select the right product.

## The thermal envelope

The thermal envelope is everything that seals conditioned air inside the home — ceiling, walls, floors, windows and doors. It should form a continuous, gap-free layer; weak spots ("thermal bridges") let heat escape or outside air in. A well-designed envelope means heating and cooling systems work more efficiently, wasting less energy and lowering bills. Related EECA guidance covers window insulation, draught reduction, and designing for energy efficiency.

## Grants

Warmer Kiwi Homes offers eligible homeowners grants of 80–90% of the cost of ceiling and underfloor insulation, and up to 80% of the cost of an energy-efficient heater. Eligibility check: https://www.eeca.govt.nz/co-funding-and-support/products/warmer-kiwi-homes-programme/check-eligibility/

## Savings methodology (as stated by EECA)

The "up to $330/year" figure uses average household energy demand from EECA's Energy End Use Database with fuel prices from 2023 MBIE data. A well-insulated home is modelled as a 50% reduction in the corresponding share of household heating energy demand. Assumptions include: heating uses either electricity or gas (not both; wood burners excluded); average thermostat setpoints of 23°C for heating and 65°C for hot water; insulation improved from the current average level to the standard for newly constructed NZ homes; figures above $50 rounded to the nearest $10.
