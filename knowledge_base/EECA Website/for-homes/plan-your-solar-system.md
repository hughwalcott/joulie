# Plan your solar system

Base the size of a home solar system on how much electricity you normally use, factoring in future needs. A system larger than needed isn't cost-effective, while one that's too small misses potential savings.

## Sizing to your usage

Your power bills show your annual usage — the more you use, the larger the system you may need. For context, the average NZ household uses about **7,000 kWh/year (around $2,400 annually)**, which aligns with a medium-sized system. Check your power company's website or app to see what time of day you use the most electricity, since solar only generates in daylight. Heavy night-time use, or a desire for energy resilience, points toward considering a battery.

## Roof suitability

- **Space** — a small system needs roof space for at least 8–12 panels (each roughly 1.6–2 m²); ground-mounting is an option with enough outdoor space, and high-efficiency panels generate more per m² where space is tight (at higher cost). Guide: **ideal** ~70 m² allows up to a 10 kW system; **good** ~35 m² allows up to 5 kW; **poor** under 20 m² is too small for even a 3 kW system.
- **Orientation** — ideal is north, northeast or northwest facing; west or east facing is good. A less-than-ideal roof feature doesn't rule solar out; the installer factors it into the design.

## Grid-connected vs off-grid

Systems can be grid-connected or independent. For most homes it's more cost-effective to keep a grid connection (unless you're somewhere a connection would have to be newly established). Grid-connected, you buy electricity when solar doesn't generate enough, and can sell unused solar back to your power company (or store it in a battery). Going fully independent needs a very large system with batteries (or a fossil-fuelled backup) to cover night-time and outages.

## Boosting savings

Adding a timer or diverter to an electric hot water cylinder is a low-cost way to boost solar savings — pre-heating water while the sun shines for use later, which cuts a large part of most power bills.

## Example household profiles

- **4 people, suburban Auckland, often home during the day, electric hot water and heating** — higher-than-average use suits a **medium** system; a battery may not be cost-effective since someone is home to use daytime solar; a hot water timer/diverter boosts savings.
- **5 people, rural Christchurch, rarely home during the day, electric hot water and heating, plus an EV** — high use and an EV suit a **large** system; because most use is at night, a **battery makes sense**.
