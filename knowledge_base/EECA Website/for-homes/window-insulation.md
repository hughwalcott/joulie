# Window insulation

Up to 30% of a home's heating energy is lost through windows, making them the biggest source of heat loss in an otherwise well-insulated home. Double glazing can reduce this to 20% or less (source cited by EECA: US Department of Energy). Options exist for most budgets: quick wins such as curtains and DIY window film make the most of existing windows, while double glazing is a larger investment that significantly improves comfort, health and energy efficiency when building new or replacing windows.

## Key features of good double glazing

Double glazing reduces heat loss, glass condensation, and heating/cooling costs, and works best alongside good home insulation. Features to look for:

- **Insulating glass units:** two panes sealed together with a gap between them.
- **Inert gas fill:** filling the gap with an insulating gas such as argon can cut window heat loss by 5–20% compared with air (source cited by EECA: BRANZ).
- **Gap size:** gaps of 12–16mm give the best insulation performance.
- **Spacers:** plastic or stainless-steel spacers around the glass edge insulate better than aluminium.
- **Low-E coating:** low-emissivity film lets light and warmth in while limiting heat escape.
- **Quality frames:** uPVC or wood frames lose less heat and attract less condensation than aluminium.

## Low-E window film

Low-E film is a micro-thin composite metal coating fitted to the inside of windows, often offered as an add-on when ordering windows. EECA notes it can reduce heat loss by up to 30% compared with regular glazing (source cited: Tintfit window films). It is virtually transparent and tint-free, can be applied to single or double glazing, and deflects incoming heat and UV, reducing overheating and fading of furnishings. Caveat: because the film reflects heat away from the glass, the glass runs colder and condensation becomes more likely — so it is best used in homes not prone to window condensation, as condensation can hinder the film's performance.

EECA's home energy efficiency expert advises that homeowners don't need to double glaze every window at once — starting with the most-used or hardest-to-heat rooms gives the biggest improvement per dollar.

## Retrofitting double glazing

Two main retrofit options:

- **Replacement windows:** the entire unit including frames is removed and replaced with new double-glazed windows (quality frames, sealed gap). Best where existing frames are at end of life.
- **Secondary glazing:** an extra pane is added to the existing window, keeping the existing frame and creating a sealed insulating gap. More cost-effective, but depends on the existing windows being in good condition.

EECA recommends professional advice and multiple quotes when comparing options.

## Curtains and blinds

Blinds and curtains reduce heat loss through single glazing, and combined with draughtproofing can offset issues with older windows. Closing curtains before dark can save NZ$80–90 per year in energy costs (EECA estimate; methodology below). The most effective curtains are double-layered with thermal linings, fit tightly against the wall or frame, are full length (touching the floor) and full width, and have pelmets fitted above. For blinds, "honeycomb" blinds are warmest — their thin tubes open when closed, creating insulating air pockets. Curtain banks may supply curtains free of charge (directory via Citizens Advice Bureau: https://www.cab.org.nz/search/curtain-bank).

## DIY window film kits

Shrink-wrap secondary-glazing kits are a low-cost DIY option that in some cases reduces heat loss almost as much as double glazing, and also reduces condensation. The film sticks to wooden window frames and is shrunk to fit with a hairdryer, creating an insulating air layer in front of the glass. Well suited to renters, limited budgets, or short-term fixes.

Practical notes: kits are sold at hardware stores and online; film is designed to last one winter but may last several years; there must be at least 5mm between the inside of the frame and the glass; frames must be clean, dry, leak-free with sound paint inside and out. Considerations: the film won't stick to aluminium frames; avoid locations where people or pets could pierce it (piercing stops it working); the tape can stain frames if left more than a season; the film is transparent but may slightly reduce view clarity.

## New builds

Most new houses and additions now require double glazing to comply with the New Zealand Building Code. New builds are an opportunity to design window size, type and placement for energy efficiency from the start (see EECA's "Design for energy efficiency" guidance).

## Savings methodology (as stated by EECA)

Curtain savings use average household energy demand from EECA's Energy End Use Database with 2023 MBIE fuel prices. Closing curtains at dusk is modelled as a 13% reduction of the corresponding share of household heating demand. Assumptions include: heating by either electricity or gas (not both; wood burners excluded); thermostat setpoints of 23°C heating / 65°C hot water; ranges in savings reflect electricity vs gas heating; figures above $50 rounded to the nearest $10.
