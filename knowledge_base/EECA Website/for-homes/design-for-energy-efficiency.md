# Design or renovate a home for energy efficiency

Energy savings can be locked in when buying, building, or renovating a home. In New Zealand the Building Code sets the minimum mandatory requirements for how homes are built; designing beyond that minimum makes a home warmer, more comfortable and more economical, can boost its value, and futureproofs it against energy supply challenges.

## Energy efficient home design

Design the home to regulate temperature efficiently using well-planned window placement, architecture, and materials that hold heat well.

### Build beyond the Building Code — Homestar

A Homestar rated home puts energy efficiency at the heart of the design: less power use via better insulation, ventilation and shading, which also help prevent dampness, mould and overheating — common problems in standard builds. A Homestar rating requires performance and sustainability standards above the NZ Building Code (details: https://nzgbc.org.nz/introduction-to-homestar).

### Energy modelling for new builds

For new builds or major renovation involving walls or windows, EECA recommends using an energy model such as the ECCHO tool from the New Zealand Green Building Council. Energy modelling predicts how a home will use energy based on its design, construction and features, and guides decisions on insulation, glazing, orientation and ventilation systems to optimise performance, comfort and return on investment (details: https://nzgbc.org.nz/energy-and-carbon-calculator-for-homes).

### Orient the home with sunlight in mind

- Orient main living areas to face north for maximum sun; put service areas (bathroom, toilet, garage) on the cooler south side.
- Well-designed roof overhangs let winter sun flood in while providing summer shade; they work best over north-facing windows. On east and west sides where the sun sits lower, deciduous trees and extendable shades or louvres work better.

### Use heat-absorbing materials (thermal mass)

Solid materials like concrete slabs absorb and store heat during the day and release it as temperatures drop at night. High-thermal-mass floors or walls in sun-filled rooms slow evening/overnight cooling and reduce heating needs. An insulated concrete floor slab can be a designed-in passive heating feature; leave sunlit areas uncovered or finished with tiles, paint or polished concrete rather than carpet so heat can be absorbed and released, and note that darker finishes absorb more solar energy.

### Choose and place windows wisely

Windows let heat in but also lose heat to colder outside air. Use larger windows on the sunny north side, smaller on east and west, and smallest on the south; place windows so air can flow through rooms for summer cooling. Choose double glazing and low-E glass (an invisible coating that admits light while trapping heat); both can be retrofitted or installed when windows are replaced.

## Build in better insulation from the start

Building or renovating is the ideal opportunity to insulate beyond minimum requirements — using the right materials and qualified professionals (see EECA's "Insulate your home" guidance).

## Big-ticket energy efficiency upgrades

Think about the home's biggest energy users and where investment returns are best.

- **Rooftop solar:** households consuming more electricity in mornings and daytime see the best financial returns from solar PV. Solar provides best value when north-facing, tilted 30–45 degrees, and unshaded. In the right circumstances panels can pay themselves off in around 8 years, then deliver low-cost energy for the next 20.
- **Ventilation systems:** all homes need ventilation to remove excess moisture. The Building Code requires extractor fans in kitchens and bathrooms; for an efficiency boost, consider heat recovery ventilation, which pre-heats incoming air by capturing heat from outgoing air.

## Efficient technology for the home

Choosing efficient appliances is one of the easiest ways to keep energy costs down — many NZ households could save over $1,000 a year by switching key appliances to efficient options.

- **Heat pumps:** highly energy efficient and cheaper to run than gas heaters, wood burners or other electric heating; an efficient heat pump can lower energy bills by around $122 per year.
- **Hot water heat pumps:** hot water heating averages about a third of a household's energy bill; a hot water heat pump could save the average household around $284 each year.
- **Induction cooktops:** the most energy-efficient cooking option on the market, and described by EECA as the safest, healthiest and fastest cooking technology available.

## Resources for designing energy efficient homes

- **Smarter Homes** (MBIE Building System Performance): guides, tools and case studies for building warmer, safer, drier homes — https://www.smarterhomes.org.nz/
- **Level** (BRANZ): evidence-based advice for designing and building homes with lower environmental impact, better health and comfort, and lower running costs — http://www.level.org.nz/
- **LCAQuick** (BRANZ): free lifecycle-assessment tool helping architects, designers and structural engineers evaluate a building design's environmental impacts — https://www.branz.co.nz/design-build/tools-and-calculators/sustainability/lcaquick
- **Passive House Institute New Zealand:** international standard for buildings needing only tiny amounts of heating/cooling; certification options for new and retrofitted houses — https://passivehouse.nz/
- **Superhome Movement:** not-for-profit raising standards so new homes are healthier and more energy efficient — https://www.superhome.co.nz/
