# Compare home water heating options

Guidance for homeowners and developers choosing a water heating system for a new build or replacement. Water heating is a major part of household energy use, so the system chosen has a big impact on running costs and emissions.

## What to weigh up

- **Energy efficiency** — a more efficient system is cheaper to run over time, so can justify a higher upfront cost.
- **Right size** — household size, number of showers/baths, and whether the system feeds the dishwasher or washing machine determine the equipment size needed.
- **Building or renovating** — group hot-water spaces together (bathroom, kitchen, toilets, laundry) so less hot water sits in pipes and it reaches taps faster.

## The options

- **Hot water heat pump** — the most efficient option, using significantly less electricity than a traditional electric system. Highest upfront cost, lowest running cost. Cheapest and most efficient when paired with rooftop solar (heating water during daylight and storing it, topping up from the grid when needed).
- **Electric resistance cylinder** — still electric, and often cheaper to install than a heat pump, but more expensive to run. Cylinders can be installed inside or outside.
- **Wetback system** — circulates water from the cylinder through pipes at the back of a firebox, using heat from a wood or pellet fire (a renewable biofuel); can be fitted to many wood/pellet fires, ranges or burners.
- **Gas (natural gas or LPG)** — fossil fuels that directly generate emissions. Cylinder and continuous-flow options exist; the most efficient is a condensing gas continuous-flow system. LPG is the most expensive on fuel cost, and gas systems carry fixed charges (mains gas supply or bottle rental) on top of energy costs.

## Running cost and emissions comparison

- A traditional hot water cylinder costs about **$480/year** to run, versus about **$180/year** for a hot water heat pump.
- Cylinders produce around **3.5 times** more carbon emissions than hot water heat pumps.
- Around **46%** of Kiwi homes with electric hot water cylinders report their system is over 10 years old; cylinders have an expected life of about 15 years, so replacement time is a good moment to switch technology rather than like-for-like.
- With rooftop solar, the cost and emissions gap between an electric resistance system and a heat pump narrows, and both are lower overall than drawing all electricity from the grid.
