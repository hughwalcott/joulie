# Reduce dampness and improve airflow

Good ventilation helps create a dry, healthy home that is more energy efficient, holds heat better, and keeps power bills down. The key is twofold: stop moisture at its source, and keep fresh air moving through the whole house. Good ventilation prevents humidity build-up, which is what allows dampness and mould to grow. Ventilation can be improved with quick fixes, or designed in when building or renovating.

## Quick wins to reduce moisture (low-cost, DIY)

- **Air the home regularly** — open doors and windows for 10–15 minutes each morning to bring in fresh air.
- **Use energy-efficient heating** — keeping rooms at a healthy, comfortable temperature (around 18°C) is the best way to reduce humidity and prevent dampness and mould.
- **Use lids on pots when cooking** — especially in homes without a rangehood.
- **Use extractor fans and rangehoods** — kitchen and bathroom fans should be big enough for the job, regularly cleaned, and vented outside — not into the ceiling space.
- **Run the bathroom fan for longer** — fit a run-on timer switch, or a humidity sensor that activates the fan when it detects steam.
- **Dry washing outside** — drying clothes indoors can release up to 5 litres of water into the home per load; use an outdoor line or a rack under a veranda, garage or carport.
- **Move furniture away from walls in winter** — prevents mould in low-airflow areas, especially along colder external walls.
- **Leave wardrobes slightly open** — improves airflow through clothing.
- **Install a shower dome** — stops excess shower steam escaping into the bathroom.
- **Use a bed base** — lifting a mattress off the floor aids circulation; children's floor beds can be raised slightly on wooden slats.

## Common sources of moisture

Persistent damp areas (often signalled by an earthy or musty smell) should be traced to their source. Common entry points, mostly easy to check:

- **Extractor fans, rangehoods and clothes dryers** — must vent outside, not indoors or into the ceiling space; ducting should be at least 150mm diameter, in good condition, and as short and straight as possible.
- **Plumbing pipes** — connections should be leak-free (common problem areas near showers/baths); checking under the house may be necessary.
- **Downpipes and gutters** — keep clear and leak-free; downpipes should connect to stormwater drains or a soakaway located away from the house.
- **Subfloor wall vents** — keep unblocked, and keep the subfloor area clear.
- **Wall and roof cladding** — check for leaks, including flashings.
- **Concrete walls and floors** — damp patches or white mineral deposits indicate moisture coming through (flooring may need lifting to check).

## Bigger jobs (may need expert help)

- **Seal damp concrete or masonry** with a waterproofing sealant or moisture barrier (seek expert product advice).
- **Improve drainage** — if surface water flows under the house in heavy rain, reshape outside ground levels or install drainage (licensed drainlayer).
- **Install subfloor vents** on all sides of the house (qualified builder for sizing/installation).
- **Measure moisture content** — a registered/accredited building surveyor can find hidden leaks by measuring material moisture (directory: https://www.boinz.org.nz/Site/accreditation/Find-an-Accredited-Building-Surveyor/).
- **Install a ground vapour barrier** — thick polythene sheeting on the ground under the house keeps moisture in the ground and stops subfloor air becoming damp.
- **Repair damage** — worn roofs, windows and walls let water creep in; key spots are roof fasteners and flashings, wall paint condition, and paint/putty on wooden windows.
- **Consider a mechanical ventilation system** — some homes, particularly airtight newer builds, need a continuous flow of fresh air.

## Mechanical ventilation systems

- **Extractor fans** — control moisture in bathrooms and kitchens; vent to the outside (never into roof spaces or cavities), use ducting of at least 150mm diameter, and keep filters clean.
- **Mechanical ventilation with heat recovery (MVHR)** — continuously replaces stale, humid indoor air with filtered fresh outdoor air, recovering heat from the outgoing air to maintain the home's temperature. EECA describes this as the best option if affordable.
- **Positive pressure ventilation systems** (e.g. DVS) — pull outside air in and push it into living areas, displacing damp air; can work well to reduce condensation, particularly in older homes.

## Rental homes

Minimum ventilation requirements apply to New Zealand rental homes under the healthy homes standards (https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/healthy-homes/ventilation-standard/). These can guide retrofits in any home, but EECA recommends exceeding them where possible.

## Relationship to the thermal envelope

Ventilation is one building block of a well-performing home alongside insulation, window insulation, draught control and energy-efficient design — all need to work together to form a continuous "seal" around the home without gaps.
