# Demand flexibility in end-use products

EECA is supporting the development and uptake of end-use products that can use electricity flexibly, across the residential, commercial and industrial sectors. This work connects to wider policy and regulatory changes progressing across government to encourage smart, flexible technology that benefits consumers and New Zealand's energy system.

## Why it matters

Flexible electricity use has the potential to fundamentally improve how New Zealanders use electricity at home and at work. If households and businesses can use technology that lets them be smart and flexible about their energy use, it could mean lower electricity bills and a more resilient, affordable and lower-emission electricity system — helping manage intermittent renewable supply (solar and wind) and peak demand (e.g. shifting load off winter evenings).

Examples of residential products that could operate flexibly include **EV chargers, heat pumps, and electric water heaters (including heat pump water heaters)**.

## Demand flexibility vs demand response

Demand flexibility involves **two-way** communication between an end-use product and an external party, enabling the product to respond to the electricity system's needs while the consumer retains control over how it operates. It should not be confused with **demand response**, which is one-way communication from the grid to a product in response to supply constraints — the classic example being ripple control that switches electric hot water cylinders off at peak times.

## Coordination and policy direction

EECA is working alongside MBIE, the Electricity Authority and the Commerce Commission, which share an expectation that flexibility will be enabled at scale. The Government has signalled potential changes to the Energy Efficiency and Conservation Act to support a competitive market for flexible electricity use — regulation extending beyond energy efficiency into enabling flexibility in technology and systems.

## Consultation

EECA consulted on a green paper at the end of 2025 (submissions closed 10 November 2025) and, in early 2026, published a summary of submissions and next steps, having refined its proposed approach to demand-flexible end-use products.
