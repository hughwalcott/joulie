# Electrification guide — Water heating

**Recommended: a hot water heat pump.**

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Average upfront cost | $4,000–10,000 installed |
| Average annual running cost (with solar) | $67 (saves ~$450/yr vs gas, ~$650/yr vs LPG) |
| Average annual running cost (without solar) | $183 (vs $529 gas, $709 LPG) |
| Emissions saved | High — ~7,000 kg over 15 years vs gas or LPG |
| Lifetime | 10–12 years |
| Saving over 15 years | ~$4,000 vs gas; ~$7,000 vs LPG |
| Rebates | No (but eligible for some low-interest green loans) |
| Install difficulty | Medium; plumber (and maybe electrician) required |
| Electrical upgrade | Maybe |

## Why it matters

Water heating is around **30% of an average home's energy load** — just behind space heating (excluding vehicles) — so it's a key appliance to electrify. It's also a big opportunity because a hot water system can act as a **"thermal battery"**: you can often choose *when* to heat the water and time it for when energy is cheapest. Paired with solar, an electric hot water system heats water at very low cost and very low emissions.

## Why a hot water heat pump

A heat pump transfers heat from the surrounding air to the water, making it very efficient. Compared with the gas or electric-resistance systems in many NZ homes, a heat pump costs more to buy but is very cheap to run.

- **Uses less energy** — typically **65–75% less** than electric-resistance or gas water heating.
- **Cheapest to run** — by far the cheapest system, and cheaper still with solar.
- **Lower emissions** — gas has the highest carbon impact; resistance uses more energy.
- **Low maintenance** — professional checks only every 3–4 years.
- **Flexible** — wifi/timer features let you heat during the day off rooftop solar.

## What to consider

Generally, more expensive heat pumps are quieter, use less electricity, have better warranties and use more environmentally friendly refrigerants.

- **Control** — a timer or wifi/wired control lets you heat off solar (best for cost and emissions).
- **Design** — split vs all-in-one (integrated) tank depends on space; a split system has a separate, larger compressor that heats faster but takes more room.
- **Efficiency** — look for a high coefficient of performance (COP); avoid models with built-in electric-resistance "back-up" elements, which draw much more energy.
- **Refrigerants** — some are potent greenhouse gases; CO₂/R-744 are good environmental choices, while R410a and R134a should be avoided.
- **Warranty** — favour longer warranties from established companies; distance from an approved service agent can add repair costs.
- **Noise** — some units are noisy (like a reverse-cycle AC); consider neighbours and window proximity, or choose a low decibel rating (e.g. 36 dB).

## Steps

1. **Plan early** — don't wait until the old heater dies; if it's near end of life (8–10 years), start researching now.
2. **Research** — check if your bank's green loan covers hot water heat pumps; find plumbers who install them (not all do yet); compare models; check electrical needs (hot water systems need their own circuit breaker, and switching off gas may need a wiring upgrade).
3. **Get quotes** from a licensed plumber who can install your preferred model.
4. **Install** — lock in your installer (some plumbers subcontract an electrician; others are licensed to do the electrical work).

## Renters

Present the information to your landlord to make the case for an upgrade; some landlords add a "comfort levy" to rent in exchange for lower bills. Using a timer to heat water — including on an electric-resistance heater — is an easy way for renters to cut bills.

## Selected FAQ

- **Getting the most from it:** install a timer/wifi control and, with solar, heat during the day when solar production peaks and the heat pump runs most efficiently.
- **Cold climates:** heat pumps work effectively even when it's snowing; better-quality units work efficiently in both cold and warm climates — avoid the cheapest versions.
- **Lifespan:** about 10–12 years.
- **Keep a resistive heater?** If you can run it off a large solar array during the day, it may make economic sense to keep it — though it still uses 3–4× more energy than a heat pump. Over 15 years, electric-resistance with solar and grid costs about the same as a hot water heat pump on the grid, including upfront costs.
- **Solar-thermal hot water?** Once the cheapest solar hot water option, but the falling cost of solar PV means it no longer is — solar-thermal is generally less efficient and takes roof space that could hold PV panels.
