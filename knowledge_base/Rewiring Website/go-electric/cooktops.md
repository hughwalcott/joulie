# Electrification guide — Cooktops

**Recommended: an induction cooktop.**

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Running cost vs gas | ~⅓ cheaper than a gas hob |
| Emissions saved | ~2 tonnes CO₂ over 15 years vs gas/LPG |
| Share of home energy | Cooking ≈ 5% of appliance energy use |
| Rebates / green loans | Generally not eligible; many retailers offer no/low-interest finance |
| Install difficulty | Moderate — certified electrician (plumber too if disconnecting gas) |
| Electrical upgrade | Maybe (some circuits/switchboard) |
| Renter option | Portable induction (~$100–200) |

## Why it matters

Cooking is a relatively small use of energy — around **5% of appliance energy** in the average NZ home — and most cooking (and refrigeration) is already electric here. But the cooktop is one of the three main gas users in a home (alongside water and space heating), and though it's the smallest, it has the most direct impact on health: cooking with gas releases toxins into the home, and in the United States one-in-eight childhood asthma cases has been linked to gas cooktops. Because cooking uses little energy, the biggest single benefit of switching the cooktop is often that it can be the **last gas appliance to go**, letting you disconnect the gas supply entirely and remove the fixed daily charge.

## Why induction

Induction cooktops use electromagnetic energy that targets the pot or pan directly — an entirely different technology to gas or electric-resistance cooktops. Compared with gas or resistive electric, induction is:

- **More efficient** — it heats the cookware directly rather than the air around it.
- **Cheaper to run** — around a third cheaper than a gas hob.
- **Safer** — the surface itself doesn't get hot, and it only heats when a pan is present.
- **Faster and easier to clean** — which is why many professional chefs have switched.

Cooking with electricity is also healthier because you're not pumping combustion toxins into the home.

## Steps

1. **Choose a model** — visit appliance stores and decide which induction cooktop you want, noting dimensions and power. Check whether you need to replace any cookware (induction needs ferrous/magnetic pots — if a magnet sticks, it works).
2. **Engage an electrician** — for advice on any additional circuits, whether the switchboard needs upgrading, and to get quotes.
3. **Disconnect gas (if applicable)** — if you're replacing a gas cooktop, a plumber will disconnect the gas and remove the old unit.
4. **Install** — your electrician makes the upgrades and installs the new cooktop.

## Renters

Renters (about a third of NZ households) miss out on the full benefits of efficient electric homes; Rewiring advocates for better renter rights, electrification finance and landlord incentives so tenants benefit. In the meantime, a **portable induction cooktop** is a great option: it sits on the bench and plugs into an existing power point (no upgrades or installation), is relatively cheap (often ~$100–200), moves house to house, and is just as efficient as an installed unit — though typically limited to one or two elements with less power. Many tenants simply place a portable induction unit over their existing gas elements. An electric barbecue is an easy way to electrify outdoor cooking too.
