# Electrification FAQ — Energy system

## Winter peaking, dry years, and intermittent solar/wind — how do we cope?

Like highways, electricity lines have short busy periods but on average run at only **30–40% capacity**. With smart demand management (charging EVs or heating water during the day) and better pricing signals that reward off-peak use and peak export, utilisation can rise substantially without over-investing in poles and wires. More customer-generated electricity means more hydro can be stored for winter peaks without burning fossil fuels — and in dry years there are typically 5–10% more sunshine hours. Electricity use is also rising in summer (air conditioning, EVs, irrigation), and solar suits that well since it generates during the day.

## Won't extra electricity demand raise bills because networks need upgrading?

The Commerce Commission sets how much the regulated network monopolies can spend, and bills are expected to rise to pay for upgrades — but Rewiring argues customer generation reduces the need. It cites a claimed **$1.2 billion win for consumers** from arguing for a greater customer role. Example: Mike Casey's Forest Lodge orchard uses ~900% more electricity after full electrification yet added no peak load (solar + battery), so no new poles or wires were needed. Much of the extra electricity NZ needs could come straight from rooftops with no network in between.

## Batteries and peak load

Just **120,000 homes** (~5% of households) with a medium battery could cut peak load by as much as the Manapouri hydro station can supply for an hour or two. Every home with a battery removes itself — and potentially its neighbours — from peak.

## Shouldn't we get people to reduce electricity consumption?

Rewiring's framing: it's **total energy** consumption, not electricity consumption, that matters for sustainability. Electrification cuts total household energy use ~70% because electric machines are 3–4× more efficient — so electric homes use more electricity but far less energy overall. Demand flexibility matters, and all customers should be rewarded for reducing demand or exporting at peak, but electrification should come first to lock in the big efficiency and emissions gains. Any further electricity savings (LED bulbs, e-bike instead of the EV) are a bonus.

## Rooftop solar vs solar farms

The 20th-century system was one-way from big generators to households. Focusing only on solar farms centralises generation "in the hands of a few companies" and raises land-use issues (though agrivoltaics show promise). Households with rooftop solar have the cheapest available energy and are a critical part of a 21st-century system: solar on all ~2.2 million homes could generate ~40% more electricity, and mid-size systems on ~50,000 farms ~60% more.
