# Explainer — Show me the money: the economics of going electric

Rewiring's framing: you will always pay for an "energy subscription," and using renewable electricity in efficient electric machines — ideally running on the sun — is the cheapest subscription you can get. Most of us instead "rent" from big energy suppliers who keep putting the rent up, when we could own cheaper energy and use as much of it as we like.

## Swap fuel for finance

The core idea is that electric machines cost more upfront but far less to run, so electrifying means swapping ongoing fuel costs for upfront finance (paid in cash if you have it, or over time like a mortgage). Solar is the clearest example — you're buying decades of energy in one go.

**Worked example (cars):** a **$52,000 EV** on a 5.5% loan costs about **$135/week** to run charging from the grid (or ~$128/week with solar), versus a **$39,000 petrol car** at about **$148/week** once maintenance and fuel are included — a saving of roughly **$11,000–16,000 over 15 years**.

Rewiring's tip: if you got a solar or battery quote a few years ago, get another — panel and battery prices have dropped around **90% in the past decade**, moving these from the most expensive sources of energy to some of the cheapest.

## Big savings for households add up to big savings for the country

Avoiding expensive, mostly imported fossil fuels and running the economy on local electrons could save New Zealand around **$29 million a day by 2040**. Rewiring's *Investing in Tomorrow* report (co-authored with the Reserve Bank's chief economist) put the cumulative figure at around **$11 billion by 2040** — which it illustrates as roughly 30 Dunedin Hospitals, 16 City Rail Links, or 380,000 Queenstown roundabouts.

## Real-world proof — Forest Lodge Orchard

Forest Lodge runs 21 electric machines and produces about 85% of the energy it needs, saving around **$40,000 a year** on diesel. It's on the (volatile) wholesale electricity rate, which suits its risk appetite — and because it has batteries to store cheap solar, it's protected when wholesale prices spike and is paid more to export at times of high demand, making the batteries a more "bankable" investment. Maniototo farmer Becks Smith (who runs Solayer, helping other farmers add solar) notes solar-plus-storage is one of the only ways farmers can cut costs without compromising on something.
