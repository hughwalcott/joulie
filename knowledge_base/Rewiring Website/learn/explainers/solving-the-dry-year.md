# Explainer — Solving the dry year

New Zealand is well-endowed with natural resources and is very reliant on **hydro storage** for electricity — so when it doesn't rain, we have to rely on other sources. This explainer asks what the best way is to plug that gap while we wait for more renewable resources to come online.

## The problem

New Zealand's hydro lakes act like a giant battery, but in a "dry year" (low rainfall, sometimes coupled with low wind) that battery runs low. To preserve water, the system typically falls back on more expensive, higher-emission generation — gas, coal, and even diesel (the Whirinaki plant) — which sets the price for the wider electricity market and pushes wholesale prices well above normal for weeks at a time. In response to faster-than-expected declines in gas supply, the major gentailers have jointly bought a "strategic reserve" of coal, and there was roughly double the coal-fired generation in 2024 compared with 2023.

## Rewiring's argument

Rewiring's consistent position is that there are **cheaper, better ways to solve the dry-year problem** than locking in new fossil infrastructure — and that an LNG import terminal is too expensive to be a sensible solution to New Zealand's diminishing domestic gas reserves. Its favoured "plug the gap" measures lean on distributed renewables and flexibility:

- **Rooftop solar is well-suited to dry years:** when it doesn't rain, it tends to shine — between April and August, sunshine hours are on average about **5% higher in a dry year** than normal. More solar keeps water in the hydro lakes for when it's most needed.
- **Every solar install displaces fossil generation:** Rewiring estimates the average install can stop roughly **one tonne of coal** from being burned at Huntly.
- **Batteries and demand flexibility** smooth the winter and dry-year peaks, reducing how often the country has to fire up coal, gas or diesel.

The through-line: build out customer-side solar, batteries and flexibility to manage dry-year and winter peaks at lower cost and lower emissions than new thermal generation or imported gas.
