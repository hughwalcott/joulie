# Explainer — A bad case of gas

Rewiring's framing: New Zealand needs gas in the short term to generate electricity and keep certain industries running, but gas is **expensive, likely to get more expensive, and running out faster than expected**. This explainer looks at how to use it now, how shortages and price rises are hitting homes and businesses, and what can replace it.

## The shortage and price spikes

Across 2024, total gas production dropped about **20%**, hitting its lowest level since 2011. In the 2025 March quarter (inflation-adjusted), gas prices were up roughly **20% (commercial), 15% (industrial) and 13% (wholesale)** year-on-year. With generators drawing on limited supply to make electricity, some industrial users can't get gas contracts at all, or face prices around three times what they pay now — NZ Hothouse's Simon Watson told RNZ some growers are in exactly that position. The strain is severe enough that some commentators describe a process of "deindustrialisation."

## The dry-year link

In 2024 — low hydro lakes, little wind, and a gas shortage all at once ("a perfect storm") — wholesale electricity prices spiked for weeks. To preserve hydro storage, the system leaned on gas, coal and even diesel (Whirinaki). Methanex sold a large chunk of its natural-gas allocation to electricity generators (reducing its own output but being paid handsomely). There was about double the coal-fired generation in 2024 versus 2023, and the major gentailers jointly bought a "strategic reserve" of coal.

## The policy backdrop (as at Sept 2025)

The explainer notes the Government put **$200 million** on the line to underwrite more oil and gas exploration and repealed the earlier exploration ban, while ministers argued for more thermal generation on reliability grounds. Rewiring's counter-position is that doubling down on fossil gas locks in cost and emissions when cheaper, cleaner alternatives exist.

## What replaces gas

- **Homes:** gas is more expensive, unhealthy and unnecessary — efficient electric equivalents (heat pumps, hot water heat pumps, induction) are cheaper to run, and new homes shouldn't be built with gas connections.
- **Industry:** many industrial users are already moving to **biomass (dried woodchips), electrode boilers, and high-temperature heat pumps**, which cover process heat below ~300°C, while higher-temperature processes still need new technology.
- **System:** more rooftop solar, batteries and demand flexibility reduce reliance on gas (and coal) for peaks and dry years.
