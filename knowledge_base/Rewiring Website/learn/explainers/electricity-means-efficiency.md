# Explainer — Electricity means efficiency

**Thesis:** electrifying everything will massively reduce the overall material and energy requirements of the global energy system. Renewable systems do take materials and energy to build, but that is dwarfed by the scale of extraction, consumption and waste in the fossil system. The piece makes its case in four parts.

## 1. Putting things in perspective (materials)

The IEA estimates critical-mineral supply must rise roughly four-fold by 2030 to stay under 1.5°C. But scale matters: in 2022 the world mined about **10 million tonnes** of minerals for low-carbon energy, versus about **15 billion tonnes** of fossil fuels (≈8.8bn coal, 4.4bn oil, ~1.8bn gas) — all single-use, straight into the atmosphere. So the fossil system's annual material demand was roughly **1,500× greater** than the renewable system's. Cumulatively to 2050, going fully renewable is estimated to need about **6.5 billion tonnes** of materials — fewer, by weight, than the coal mined in a single year. The conclusion: while demand for *some* minerals rises, total mining falls far below the status quo.

Locally: electrifying all private vehicles would need about **760,000 tonnes** of materials for EV batteries (the part that differs from a petrol car), while *avoiding* the import of about **3.1 million tonnes of fossil fuels per year** (~2.7 billion litres of petrol and 1.1 billion litres of diesel), plus 68 fuel-ship visits and ~300,000 tanker-truck trips.

## 2. Less energy, more electricity (efficiency)

Fossil fuels must be burned to be useful, and most primary energy is lost as heat; the global system converts only about **35%** of primary energy into useful energy. A petrol car turns just **16–25%** of petrol's energy into motion; an induction stove uses **half** the energy of gas to cook the same meal. Around two-thirds of the ~15 billion tonnes of fossil fuel produced each year is wasted — yet still emits CO₂ (global fossil CO₂ ≈ 37 billion tonnes; production and transport alone > 5 billion tonnes). New Zealand spends about **$20 billion a year** on fossil fuels; globally the wasted energy is worth around **US$4.6 trillion a year** (~5% of global GDP).

Renewables are different: a solar panel or turbine is a one-off commitment of materials with no ongoing fuel, and it produces electricity *directly*, avoiding the conversion losses that make fossil fuels (and hydrogen) inefficient. This is why we don't need to replace all current *primary* energy with renewable electricity — the "primary energy fallacy." An electrified global system would be about **40% more energy-efficient**, needing 40% less primary energy for the same services; at the machine level, an EV is about **4× more efficient** than a combustion car.

## 3. Less waste, more space

Extracting less means wasting less. If the world deploys enough solar for net-zero, cumulative solar-panel waste to 2050 might be **54–160 million tonnes** — versus **45.5 billion tonnes** of coal ash under business-as-usual (300–800× greater). Put another way, all the solar waste to 2050 ≈ the coal ash produced globally *each month*. Renewables can also use less land if we prioritise "people-sized" assets like rooftop solar (negligible land) and agrivoltaics; small-to-mid-scale generation on homes and farm buildings alone could **double New Zealand's renewable capacity**, with fewer land-use conflicts and less community resistance than large plants.

## 4. Many happy returns (EROI)

On Energy Return On Investment (energy produced ÷ energy to build and run): solar PV ranges **10–60**, wind **≥25** (NZ's Harapaki wind farm assessed at **66**), all well clear of the "energy cliff," and improving over time — while fossil-fuel EROI is in irreversible decline. Pessimistic analyses usually compare fossil energy at the *point of extraction* against renewables at the *point of use*, confusing primary with useful energy; comparing like-with-like, renewable electricity comes out ahead.

## "More with less"

The transition isn't free of trade-offs — near-term bottlenecks in some critical minerals, the need for low-impact mining and circular value chains, and real humanitarian issues in supply chains (e.g. Xinjiang polysilicon, DRC cobalt). But the fossil system has all these problems at far greater scale, and the transition is a chance to do better. Electrification needs more electricity but **less energy and fewer materials** — "the epitome of doing more with less."
