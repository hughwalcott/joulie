# Explainer — Closing the Loop

**Thesis:** an electrified economy is a *more* circular economy. Solar panels and batteries do reach end-of-life, but an electrified energy system is full of opportunities for redesign, reuse, recovery and recycling — and a flood of e-waste is both avoidable and against our interests to allow (countries that landfill valuable materials risk being shut out of clean-tech markets, because manufacturers want those resources back).

## What the circular economy is

Against the linear "take-make-waste" model, a circular economy aims to eliminate waste and pollution, circulate products and materials, and regenerate nature. Globally the share of secondary (reused) materials was only **7.2% in 2023** (down from 9.1% in 2018). The fossil system is inherently linear — ~15 billion tonnes of fuel moved per year plus ~1.1 billion tonnes of toxic coal ash — whereas renewable-energy waste flows are far smaller and far more recoverable (solar-panel material recovery is already **>90%**).

## Closed-loop systems

Two loops need closing: the new materials the transition mobilises (battery lithium, panel metals) and the existing materials in fossil machines headed for landfill. The old fossil system can effectively be "mined": there's about **460 million tonnes of copper** already in use globally, recyclable indefinitely — recovering it could meet ~40% of the transition's copper needs. For EV lithium-ion batteries, recycling already achieves **>90% extraction** of lithium, cobalt, nickel and manganese, with best-in-class rates **>99.6% for cobalt and nickel and >95% for lithium**. The Energy Transitions Commission expects that by **2050 more than half** the demand for cobalt, graphite and lithium could be met by recycled secondary supply (some expect sooner). Electrifying NZ's private vehicle fleet would import ~760,000 tonnes of battery materials — which we need to channel back to manufacturers. Local mover **Phoenix Metalman** already recovers ~98% of solar-panel materials; Australia is further ahead (several states have banned solar panels from landfill; a third of homes have rooftop solar versus under 3% here).

## Smart materials, circular business models, behaviours

- **Smart materials:** grown or low-harm materials — e.g. NZ's **CarbonScape** turning wood into biographite for EV batteries, Sweden's **Modvion** building wooden wind-turbine towers, and perovskite "solar paint"; plus avoiding "forever chemicals" (PFAS), for which safer alternatives already exist.
- **Circular business models** (the OECD's five): circular supply, resource recovery, **product life extension** (EV batteries keep 70–80% capacity at end of warranty — ideal for home storage; plus e-retrofitting diesel vehicles), **sharing of assets**, and **products-as-services** (subscription appliances, Energy-as-a-Service like SolarZero).
- **Regenerative behaviours:** recycling and supporting circular start-ups and community energy — a logic that resonates with the whakapapa-centred thinking of mātauranga Māori — while avoiding the degenerative harms (destructive mining, human-rights abuses) of the old system.
- **Network participation & embedded intelligence:** smart meters, peer-to-peer trading and data traceability can turn "supply chains into supply circles."

## Where to from here

Rewiring proposes three moves: a coordinated **circular energy roadmap**; stronger **product stewardship / Extended Producer Responsibility** (building on the Waste Minimisation Act 2008 and MfE's priority-product work); and targeted support for **circular innovation** (modelled on things like the Plastics Innovation Fund). The point: approach the transition with a circular mindset, not a linear one — it's avoidable waste, and a genuine source of innovation and prosperity.
