# Explainer — Sun in a socket: plug-in solar

Solar is the fastest-growing source of electricity globally and the cheapest form available to NZ households — but not every home is suitable for rooftop panels, not everyone owns their home, and not everyone can access the capital for a full rooftop system. In several countries this has driven a surge in small-scale **plug-in solar**. This explainer asks why it hasn't taken off in New Zealand yet.

## The equity gap

About one-third of NZ households rent, and housing is getting denser (apartments, townhouses, flats). Rooftop solar may not appeal to debt-averse or low-income households, and some roofs are unsuitable. Yet **lower-income households spend around five times more on energy as a proportion of their income** than the highest-income households — so those with the most to gain from lower bills are often the least able to access the technology that would deliver it.

## What plug-in solar is

Plug-in solar (also called portable or "balcony" solar) is a small photovoltaic system — typically one or two panels paired with a microinverter — that connects to a home through a **standard wall socket**. Unlike rooftop solar, it requires no modification to the property, no shared-ownership model, and no landlord permission. It can power things like a fridge or laptop and move house to house with the tenant.

## The regulatory contrast

Rewiring notes that in Germany and the UK, **landlords are legally prohibited from unreasonably refusing a tenant's request to install a qualifying device**, with the burden of proof on the landlord to show specific grounds for refusal — a model that has helped plug-in solar scale overseas.

## How it fits Rewiring's wider argument

For renters, installing *rooftop* solar requires landlord consent and a mutually beneficial arrangement to pay for it (the Queenstown Electrification Accelerator's "Solar for Renters" scheme shows this can be a win-win). Plug-in solar sidesteps all of that. But the biggest barrier to household electrification generally remains the upfront cost — which is why Rewiring argues long-term, low-interest energy loans are "the ladder we need to get more people over that."
