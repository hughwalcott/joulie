# Electrification FAQ — Vehicles

## Reliability, longevity and maintenance

A German Automobile Club study found EVs averaged 3.8 breakdowns per 1,000 vehicles, less than half the rate of similarly aged combustion cars. With no oil changes and fewer moving parts, EVs have lower maintenance and repair costs. Modern EVs now have similar lifespans to combustion cars (~18 years) and higher lifetime mileage, and EV batteries are often outlasting the cars.

## Range, charging and "do we need lots of fast chargers?"

NZ doesn't need to replace petrol stations. Fast charging matters for long trips and for those without off-street parking, but rolling out cheaper, convenient **slow** chargers (supermarkets, beaches, parks, workplaces) makes more sense and has minimal network impact. Many new EVs have 500km+ range; the average NZ car drives ~11,000 km/year (~200 km/week), which is roughly four hours a week on a 7 kW charger — easily covered by overnight home charging. Around 85% of charging happens at home, and ~60% of owners trickle-charge on a standard plug (adding ~100 km of range overnight).

## Do you need a special charger?

No — any three-pin household socket works, just slower. Cheap timers (or the car's schedule) capture off-peak rates. Older homes should check wiring/switchboard, as prolonged charging can heat up. Smart chargers charge faster and can divert solar, but cost more.

## Costs and road user charges (RUCs)

Even with RUCs, EVs are much cheaper than fossil-fuel cars, especially on rooftop solar. On a petrol-equivalent basis you can power an EV for about **$0.30/L with rooftop solar** and **$0.60/L from the grid** — fast charging costs more but is still cheaper than petrol. Everyone should pay their fair share for road use.

## Resale values and buying

EV prices have dropped as early-adopter premiums faded and technology improved, which is good for second-hand buyers. Global forecasts suggest EVs could reach price parity with fossil-fuel cars around 2026–2027. A petrol car may be cheaper upfront but locks in years of rising fuel costs.

## Do EVs make sense in the country?

Even more so — rural drivers cover more distance (sometimes 50,000+ km/year), and the more you drive, the more you save. Over 90% of charging is at home, and 500km+ ranges mean fast chargers are rarely needed.

## Battery degradation and cold weather

Modern EV batteries are guaranteed for many years (EECA notes ~8–10 years or ~160,000 km — similar to 15 years of average driving) and show small degradation, generally lasting the vehicle's life. Fast charging has little impact on most modern EVs (which have active thermal management). Cold below freezing can cut range 10–20% and slow charging, but with average driving ~210 km/week, that's rarely a practical issue; preconditioning while plugged in helps.

## Hybrids — a good starting point?

Rewiring's position: if you can, go straight to a full EV. Standard hybrids (HEVs) still burn ~70% of the petrol (and emissions) of a petrol car and aren't low-emissions vehicles. Plug-in hybrids (PHEVs) can drive some distance emission-free but their real emissions depend on whether owners charge them; PHEVs with 100–200 km battery range may have a short-term role, especially for utes with few full-EV options — but they're a stepping stone, not the solution.

## Vehicle-to-everything (V2X): using an EV as a battery

Technologies like vehicle-to-home (V2H) and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) turn EVs into storage. It's estimated that if 30% of NZ cars were electric, their V2G output could equal all NZ's power stations at full power combined. A typical EV battery (60+ kWh) can power a small home for about a week. Some EVs already do vehicle-to-load (V2L) — running appliances or tools. Rewiring's view is that the main blocker isn't NZ regulations or the grid but car manufacturers not yet unlocking the capability; bidirectional chargers (e.g. Sigenergy, StarCharge Halo) are appearing, and Nissan/Mitsubishi have unlocked it on some cars (cheap second-hand Nissan Leafs could especially help lower-income households). It's not here at scale yet, but it's getting closer.

## Are EVs safe (fires)?

Australian project EV FireSafe found, from 2010–2020 global data, roughly a **0.0012%** chance of a passenger EV battery catching fire, versus country-based estimates of around **0.1%** for internal-combustion vehicles.

## Towing

Towing reduces range depending on load and vehicle design (one test showed an Audi e-tron losing ~a third towing a caravan; a Tesla Model 3 ~half). Most EVs in NZ today aren't designed for heavy towing, though this is changing.

## What about hydrogen?

Think of hydrogen as a (poor) battery, not an energy source: making hydrogen from electricity and turning it back into electricity returns only about a third of the energy, so it's less efficient and dearer for customers than just using electricity or a battery. There may be a role in heavy transport, steel and cement, but not for light vehicles — we already have a nationwide electricity grid and growing home solar.
