# Electrification FAQ — Upfront costs, loans and savings

## Electricity is already expensive — why electrify?

Even getting all their electricity from the grid (rather than solar), electric homes still save considerably versus fossil-fuelled homes. Generating your own via rooftop solar lowers costs further because it's consumed where it's produced.

## The upfront cost is higher — is it worth it?

Electric machines usually cost more upfront but are much cheaper — and more price-stable — over the long run. A fossil-fuel machine may be cheaper to buy but commits you to years of volatile, rising fuel prices. Rewiring argues NZ has reached an "electrification tipping point": households and businesses can save money now by electrifying, even with upfront costs and finance built in. The framing they use is "swapping fuel for finance."

## How much can the average home save?

On average, homes using gas appliances and petrol vehicles could save around **$1,500/year** (or around **$4,500/year** with a low-interest loan) by switching to electric equivalents on a mix of solar, battery and grid.

## What finance options are available?

If you have the money, paying upfront and capturing the bill savings is best; solar can provide a 10–15% return on investment (better than typical deposit rates) and lock in electricity prices for the panels' lifetime. Otherwise:

- Talk to your lender about adding solar to your mortgage or their green finance products.
- All major banks offer low-interest **green loans** to home-loan customers (ASB, BNZ, Kiwibank, Westpac, ANZ and others), though these are generally short-term. MoneyHub summarises the options (mortgage top-ups, green loans, green credit cards).

*(Rewiring doesn't endorse specific products and recommends consulting a financial advisor.)*

## What is Rewiring advocating for, and what about low-income households?

Access to finance is a key barrier, so Rewiring advocates for **low-interest, long-term loans for household electrification** — a government-backed line of credit, indexed to inflation, secured on the property and repaid when it sells — available to homeowners and landlords for solar, batteries, EV chargers and replacing gas appliances. This would come at minimal or zero net cost to government over time. Rewiring also supports electrification **grants or subsidies for low-income households**, who benefit even more (they spend a greater share of income on energy). Its "Investing in Tomorrow" paper frames electrification as an investment that could save households $29 million per day by 2040 by avoiding imported fossil fuels.
