# Electrification FAQ — Solar

## Does New Zealand get enough sunlight for rooftop solar?

Yes. NZ's sunlight resource is similar to the Australian state of Victoria (which has ~30–35% solar penetration, versus NZ's ~3%), and better than Germany, which has almost five million solar sites and got 14% of its electricity from solar in 2024. NZ enjoys 1,600–2,500 sunlight hours a year depending on region. Panels still generate when cloudy, and with falling costs it can now be economical to put panels even on south-facing or shady roofs. Check viability with NIWA SolarView or the Gen Less solar calculator.

## Is it worth getting just solar, with no battery?

Absolutely. Rooftop solar is the cheapest delivered electricity available to NZ households — on average about **13c per unit** including upfront costs and interest on a loan, versus around **35c** from the grid. Using solar during the day saves the most (e.g. heating water — around 30% of a home's electricity use — running the dishwasher, washing machine, or charging an EV). Excess can be exported. A resistive electric cylinder plus solar can act as a "thermal battery," timed to heat during the day.

## How much does it cost?

Installed solar costs around **$2,000/kW** (a bit more for smaller installs, less for larger), varying by region, with installers often running deals. A solar system needs a smart meter, and an old switchboard may need replacing — your installer advises as part of the quote.

## What should I consider?

- **Inverter type** — microinverters (one per panel) are pricier but more effective and good for shady/complex roofs; string inverters connect panels together.
- **Quality** — better panels have higher efficiency and longer warranties.
- **System size** — if you can, install the biggest system that fits and you can afford, to cover future needs (like an EV); it's cheaper to install all at once. (The average Australian home system is ~9 kW, enough for a household plus two EVs.)

## Rooftop solar vs solar farms / large-scale renewables

Rewiring argues rooftop solar cuts out the "middleman": energy is generated where it's consumed, so there are no distribution (poles-and-wires) costs — and distribution is more than half of what customers pay. Even a hypothetical free central power plant would be dearer to customers than rooftop solar once network costs and margins are included. Solar on all ~2.2 million homes could generate ~40% more electricity; mid-size systems on ~50,000 farms ~60% more. AUT academics noted the 14 biggest Auckland rooftops equal NZ's largest solar farm. Large solar farms also raise land-use issues (though agrivoltaics show promise).

## We already have an 80%+ renewable grid — why do we need rooftop solar?

Electricity is only part of the energy system — around 70% of NZ's total energy use is still fossil fuels (transport, heating, industry). Electricity demand is expected to double or triple as the economy electrifies, and the lowest-cost way to supply much of that is rooftop solar and batteries, which also keeps water in the hydro dams for winter and reduces fossil-fuel generation.

## Exporting excess solar

Any excess you don't use is fed back to the grid (unless you have a battery). The buy-back / feed-in rate varies considerably between retailers, so shop around. The biggest savings come from using your own solar as it's produced, so set timers and run appliances/chargers during the day. Rewiring advocates for **Symmetrical Export Tariffs** — paying customers fairly for exporting at peak — to make batteries more "bankable."

## Will solar work in a blackout?

Grid-connected systems usually shut down during an outage for safety. But some inverters can still supply a small amount of solar to the home when the grid is down, and a home battery can back up the house. If you want this, specify it to your installer.

## Roof suitability and warranties

Installers say very few roofs are unsuitable (only ~5%). If a roof is near end of life, consider replacing it before installing. Solar generally won't void a roof warranty if installed to the manufacturer's guidelines, and reputable installers use proper mounting, flashing and sealing to avoid leaks.

## Old panels — what happens to them?

Material recovery rates for solar panels are already well over 90% (local company Phoeniix Metalman recovers ~98%). Even with a massive global rollout, plausible solar waste to 2050 is roughly equal to the coal ash produced globally each month.
