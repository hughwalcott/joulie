# Electrification FAQ — Farms

## What is an electric farm?

Modern farming relies heavily on diesel machines (tractors, motorbikes, pumps, harvesters). An electric farm swaps some or all of those for electric equivalents powered by solar panels, battery banks and the grid. With enough generation, electric farms can also sell renewable electricity to the grid at peak demand, creating a new revenue stream.

## What role can farms play in the energy system?

Farms are well placed for mid-scale renewable generation: more buying power than households, land and space for larger systems, owner-operators who can decide, and access to finance for long-term investments. If each of NZ's ~50,000 farms installed mid-scale solar, they could generate an additional ~60% of NZ's current total electricity consumption. Solar also suits irrigation (which lifts summer demand) and can help keep hydro lakes full for winter.

## How much can farms save?

Every farm differs, but the agriculture sector spends around **$700 million on diesel each year**. Electric machines cost more upfront but are much cheaper to run; with solar and batteries, farms can cut energy bills and improve resilience, and electrifying fossil-fuel machines reduces on-farm emissions and reliance on carbon offsetting.

## Example — Forest Lodge Orchard

The Central Otago orchard electrified all its on-farm machinery (including two electric frost-fighting fans, electric golf carts and NZ's first electric tractor). Rooftop and ground-mount solar plus a battery bank provides about 80% of the farm's electricity, so although electricity use rose ~900%, peak grid drawdown stayed low — no new local network infrastructure was needed. The farm saves almost **$40,000/year** across farm and home from avoided diesel, avoided lines charges and exporting at high prices. Its two 30 kW electric frost fans run at about 20% of the operational cost of diesel fans, and a 40 hp electric tractor does the work of an 80 hp diesel at a fraction of the running cost.
