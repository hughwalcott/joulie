# Report — Electric Homes (2024)

Rewiring Aotearoa's flagship report, released 18 March 2024, is the analytical basis for much of its household guidance. Its central finding is that New Zealand is **one of the first countries in the world to reach the "electrification tipping point"** — the point at which a typical household can *both* save money *and* cut emissions by switching its appliances and vehicles to electric, even after upfront costs and loan finance are included.

## Headline savings

On average, homes currently using gas or LPG appliances and petrol vehicles could save:

- **over $1,485 per year** by switching to electric equivalents supplemented by rooftop solar and a home battery, assuming a **5.5%** loan rate; and
- up to **$4,699 per year** with low-interest financing at **1%**.

(Rewiring rounds these to roughly **$1,500** and **$4,500** in its general communications.) Homes that use more power, hot water and vehicle travel save the most, and electrification can bring home energy emissions close to zero.

## The solar economics at the core

Over a 30-year term, the report models rooftop solar producing electricity at around **$0.06/kWh** financed at 0% interest, or about **$0.12/kWh** at a 5.5% finance rate — versus an average NZ grid price of around **$0.34/kWh**. This is why Rewiring calls rooftop solar the cheapest form of delivered energy available to NZ homes, with widespread adoption able to double or triple renewable generation.

## Emissions significance

Reviewing the report, Professor Shaun Hendy (MNZM FRSNZ) called it "an important and timely report that deserves considerable attention," noting it finds electrification "financially favourable for a typical New Zealand household today, taking into account the current cost of capital." He estimated that household electrification could eliminate approximately **10% of the current national greenhouse gas inventory** — significant, yet (he noted) barely accounted for in New Zealand's Emissions Reduction Plan — and that the impact of local supply via rooftop solar plus batteries has been greatly underestimated.

## Context and framing

The report situates electrification in NZ's electrical heritage: the world's first all-electric home and first practical electric home water heater (engineer Lloyd Mandeno, early in NZ's hydro boom); the southern hemisphere's first public electric lighting in Reefton (~1888); the world's first electric gold dredge (~1890); the world's first wet-steam geothermal power station (1958); and, more recently, what's believed to be the world's first zero-fossil-fuel fruit orchard (Forest Lodge, Central Otago). Rewiring argues this heritage, plus a highly renewable grid, positions NZ to become one of the world's most electric economies.

*(Cost of electric machines — EVs, appliances, solar, batteries — has fallen substantially over the past decade; the report notes major banks have begun offering electrification finance but that broader access is needed.)*

## 2025 update — savings have roughly doubled

In 2025 Rewiring refreshed the model (reviewed by independent economist Cameron Bagrie). Where the 2024 figure was ~$1,500/year, the updated modelling finds a household could now save **up to ~$3,000/year over 15 years** (a net saving of about **$45,000**), or about **$7,600/year** on bills if the upfront costs are removed. Scaled across the country's stock of appliances and vehicles as homes electrify over time, that's a net **~$64 billion over 15 years, reaching ~$9.1 billion/year by 2040**, and about **8.7 million fewer tonnes of CO₂-equivalent emissions per year by 2040**. Rewiring stresses it is *not* advocating replacing working appliances all at once — CEO Mike Casey frames the ask as every New Zealander making "**six good decisions in the next 10 years**," i.e. choosing electric each time a machine needs replacing. The update also underpins Rewiring's support (with Local Government NZ) for a **Ratepayer Assistance Scheme** / "home energy fund" offering low-interest electrification loans to all homeowners regardless of mortgage status, with attention to not leaving renters and low-income households behind.
