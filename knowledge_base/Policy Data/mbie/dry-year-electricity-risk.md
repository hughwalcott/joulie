# Managing dry-year electricity risk

## The problem

New Zealand's electricity supply depends heavily on hydro. In **dry years**, low hydro inflows mean less energy is available, and recent changes — less thermal generation, aging thermal plant, declining domestic gas, and more weather-dependent wind and solar — have increased the system's exposure to weather. New Zealand's wholesale market is an **"energy-only" market**: generators are paid when they generate, not for keeping plant and fuel available, so costly plant that only runs in rare dry years may be under-incentivised. The 2024 winter demonstrated the risk.

## The government's approach (replacing Lake Onslow)

Rather than a single large storage project (Lake Onslow was scrapped in December 2023), policy has shifted toward faster consenting for a broad range of renewables **plus stronger reliability rules**. As at mid-2026:

- MBIE consulted on a **proposed two-layer reliability obligation** to strengthen incentives for market participants to secure enough long-duration firm energy to get the system through a dry winter. **Submissions closed 21 July 2026.**
- **Cabinet agreed** to a strengthened regulatory framework, to be progressed through amendments to the **Electricity Industry Act 2010** and the **Government Policy Statement on Energy**.
- This sits within a broader **Energy Package**, which also includes progressing procurement of a **liquefied natural gas (LNG) import facility** to firm supply, and expectations that the System Operator publish an Electricity Security Opportunities Statement and MBIE update its demand/generation scenarios at least every three years.

## ⚠ Currency note

This policy area was **under active consultation as of mid-2026** — the reliability obligation was a proposal, not finalised policy, when this record was made. Check for the finalised obligation, the Electricity Industry Act amendments, and the status of the LNG import facility before stating any of these as settled. This is an appropriate area to hedge.
