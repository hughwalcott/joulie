# Keeping the lights on (security of supply)

**Security of supply** is the ability of electricity supply to meet demand over time: enough **generation** each day to meet peak demand ("capacity" security), and enough **fuel** to generate over the longer term ("energy" security). Demand is highest in cold winter evenings when people get home, heat up and cook.

## What the Authority monitors

- **Hydro storage and inflows** — hydro is around **60%** of New Zealand's supply, but the lakes are shallow (they could go from full to empty in about six weeks without rain), so storage and inflows are watched constantly.
- **Wind and solar** — these only generate when the wind blows or sun shines, so output is forecast to keep the system running smoothly.
- **Thermal fuel availability** — gas, coal and diesel act as backup when renewables aren't producing enough.
- **New generation** — how many new wind/solar farms, rooftop solar systems and batteries are planned, to gauge whether there'll be enough electricity in future.

## How risk is managed

The system operator compares stored water against **energy risk curves**; as lake levels fall into **watch, alert or emergency** zones, the security-of-supply level rises and action is taken. If the risk of a shortage exceeds ~10% for at least a week, the system operator may declare an **official conservation campaign** asking the public to voluntarily reduce use. **Rolling outages** (compulsory) are a last resort to prevent complete system failure. The Code also requires retailers to run a **customer compensation scheme**, paying qualifying customers a minimum weekly amount for reducing use during an official compensation campaign.

## Who does what

Keeping the lights on is a shared job: the **Electricity Authority** sets the rules and incentives so participants invest in and manage supply; the **system operator** (Transpower) runs the system in real time; and **generators and retailers** invest and manage risk. The Authority publishes a **weekly security-of-supply snapshot** covering fuel stocks, national hydro storage, forecast rainfall, unavailable infrastructure, and spot and forward prices.

## The future

Increasingly, more homes, businesses and communities will produce and manage their own electricity (rooftop solar and batteries), making communities more **resilient** in major weather events and natural disasters and letting people benefit from supplying power into their local network.
