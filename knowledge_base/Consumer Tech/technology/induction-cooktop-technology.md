# How an induction cooktop works

## The electromagnetic principle
Beneath the glass surface is a tightly wound **coil of metal**. When switched on, an **alternating current** flows through the coil, creating a **magnetic field** above it — the cooktop surface itself stays cool. When a pan with a suitable **magnetic, electrically-resistive (iron-based) base** is placed on it, the field induces circulating **"eddy currents"** directly within the pan's base; the pan's resistance turns those currents into heat within the pan itself (a related effect, magnetic hysteresis, also contributes). Heat then transfers to the food by conduction and convection.

## Why only certain cookware works
Induction needs a pan base that is magnetic and resistive. **Aluminium, copper, glass and ceramic** are either non-magnetic or conduct too efficiently to generate useful heat, so they won't heat on induction unless they have a magnetic base layer bonded in. Compatible materials: **cast iron, enamelled cast iron, carbon steel, magnetic ("18/0"-type) stainless steel**, and nonstick pans with a magnetic base.

## How to check existing pans
Use the **magnet test**: hold a fridge magnet to the base of the pot — if it sticks firmly, the base has enough magnetic material to work on induction. Many compatible pans are also marked with a small **coiled-spring / spiral symbol**.
