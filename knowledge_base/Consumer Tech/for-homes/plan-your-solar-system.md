# Plan your home solar system

## Grid-connected vs off-grid
- **Grid-connected (grid-tied):** stays linked to the network — draws power from the grid when solar isn't generating enough (e.g. at night) and can **export surplus back to the grid, often for a credit**. EECA notes this is the more cost-effective option for most homes.
- **Off-grid:** no grid connection; must generate and store all its own power, needing a much larger array plus a substantial battery bank (or fossil-fuel backup).

## Hybrid systems and batteries
A **hybrid** system is grid-connected but also includes a **battery**, storing daytime solar for evening/overnight use while still able to draw from or export to the grid. EECA's guidance: a battery is most worthwhile for households that **use a lot of power at night** or want **backup during outages**; for households mostly using power in daylight, a battery is generally less cost-effective (solar can be used directly as generated).

## Selling excess back
With a typical grid-connected setup a household can **sell surplus solar back to its power company** — the buy-back rate varies by retailer and plan.

## What size system to install
EECA recommends considering: current electricity **usage** (from past bills); **future needs** (EV, more occupants); **roof** space, **orientation** (north-facing is ideal in New Zealand), tilt, condition and shading; **daytime occupancy** (solar only generates in daylight); and whether a **battery** is worthwhile (mainly depends on night vs day use).

## Orientation and tilt
Because New Zealand is in the Southern Hemisphere, a **north-facing** roof receives the most sun over a day. Orientation, tilt angle and shading are the key factors — a well-oriented, unshaded array produces significantly more than an identical south-facing or shaded one.
